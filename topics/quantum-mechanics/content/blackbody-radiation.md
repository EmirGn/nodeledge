## The idea

Heat any object — a poker, a star, a stovetop coil — and it glows. Cool objects glow in invisible infrared; hotter ones shift to red, then orange, then white-hot blue. A "blackbody" is an idealized perfect absorber and emitter, and the spectrum of light it radiates depends on nothing but its temperature. That universality is a gift to physicists: one clean curve to explain.

## Why it matters

Classical physics tried to explain that curve and produced a disaster. Treating the light inside a hot cavity as a collection of waves that could hold any amount of energy, the theory predicted that shorter wavelengths (toward the ultraviolet) should carry more and more energy without limit. A warm oven should blast you with infinite ultraviolet light. This absurd prediction was dubbed the **ultraviolet catastrophe**. Reality does the opposite: the curve rises, peaks, and falls back to zero at short wavelengths.

In 1900 Max Planck found the fix, but only by making a strange assumption he called an act of desperation: the oscillators in the cavity walls can't emit energy in any amount. They emit it in discrete packets. That single move — energy is *quantized* — is the seed from which all of quantum mechanics grows.

## The math (light)

Planck's radical ingredient was that a packet of light energy is proportional to its frequency:

**E = h · f**

- **E** — energy of one packet (later called a photon)
- **f** — frequency of the light (how fast it oscillates)
- **h** — Planck's constant, a tiny fixed number (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ joule-seconds) that sets the "grain size" of energy in the universe

Because high-frequency packets are expensive, the cavity rarely has enough energy to make them — and the ultraviolet catastrophe vanishes.

## See it

Watch a blacksmith heat iron. As temperature climbs, the glow marches from dull red through orange to yellow-white — the peak of the emission curve sliding toward shorter wavelengths. The same physics tells us a star's color reveals its surface temperature: red giants are cool, blue-white stars are scorching. Every one of those glows traces Planck's curve, not the classical one that would have set the sky on fire.
