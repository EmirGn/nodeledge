## The idea

Richard Feynman called this "the only mystery" of quantum mechanics. Shoot particles — photons, electrons, even whole molecules — at a barrier with two narrow slits, and let them hit a screen behind it. Send them one at a time, so no particle can bump into another. Each lands as a single dot. But as the dots accumulate, they don't pile up behind the two slits as bullets would. They form alternating bright and dark stripes: an interference pattern. Each particle, alone, somehow behaves as a wave passing through *both* slits and interfering with itself.

## Why it matters

This is duality caught in a single decisive act, and it forces the wavefunction on us. A lone particle doesn't have a definite path; it has a spread-out wave of possibilities that explores both routes, and the two routes' amplitudes add and cancel. The killer twist: install a detector to learn which slit each particle *actually* went through, and the interference pattern collapses into two ordinary bumps. Gaining "which-path" information destroys the wave behavior. This is not about clumsy equipment or a conscious watcher — it's that entangling the particle with any which-path record removes the possibility of interference. Measurement fundamentally changes the outcome.

## The math (light)

Waves interfere where their peaks align. Bright bands appear where the path-length difference from the two slits is a whole number of wavelengths:

**d · sin θ = m · λ**

- **d** — distance between the two slits
- **θ** (theta) — angle to a bright band on the screen
- **m** — an integer (0, 1, 2, …) labeling which band
- **λ** (lambda) — the particle's wavelength (its de Broglie wavelength)

## See it

Picture ripples from two stones dropped in a pond: where crests meet crests, water heaves high; where crest meets trough, the surface goes flat. The screen's stripes are exactly this — but assembled one dot at a time by particles that arrive individually. The pattern lives in the statistics of thousands of lonely, particle-like hits.
