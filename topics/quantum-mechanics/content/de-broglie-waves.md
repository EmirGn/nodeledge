## The idea

In 1924 Louis de Broglie made a daringly symmetric leap. Physics had just accepted that light — long thought a pure wave — also behaves like particles. De Broglie flipped it: if waves are secretly particles, then particles must secretly be waves. Every electron, proton, atom, and yes, every baseball has an associated wavelength. We never notice matter waves in daily life because the wavelength shrinks as momentum grows, and everyday objects have enormous momentum compared to Planck's tiny constant. An electron, being featherlight, has a wavelength comparable to the spacing of atoms — big enough to matter.

## Why it matters

This is the second half of duality, and it universalizes the quantum. It means the wave equations we'll soon write down aren't a quirk of light; they govern all matter. It explains why electrons in atoms sit in specific, stable orbits: an orbit only survives if the electron's wave closes smoothly on itself, like a standing wave on a ring, ruling out all the in-between energies. That standing-wave condition is the reason atoms have discrete energy levels — and the reason chemistry exists. De Broglie's wavelength is the launching point for Schrödinger's whole framework.

## The math (light)

De Broglie recycled the same bridge equation, now applied to matter:

**λ = h / p**

- **λ** (lambda) — the matter wavelength of the object
- **h** — Planck's constant, extremely small
- **p** — the object's momentum (mass times velocity)

A thrown baseball has a wavelength around 10⁻³⁴ meters — unmeasurably small, so it looks perfectly particle-like. A slow electron has a wavelength near 10⁻⁹ meters — atomic scale, so its waviness is unmissable.

## See it

In 1927 Davisson and Germer fired electrons at a nickel crystal and found them bouncing off in a pattern of bright and dim directions — an interference pattern, exactly as X-ray *waves* would produce. Electrons, unmistakably particles a moment earlier, were diffracting like light. Today electron microscopes exploit these short matter wavelengths to image viruses and atoms, resolving detail far finer than any light microscope ever could.
