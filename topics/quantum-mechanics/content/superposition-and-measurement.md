## The idea

A quantum system doesn't have to pick a state and stick with it. An electron's spin can be "up," "down," or any weighted combination of both at once — a **superposition**. This is not ignorance about a hidden truth; the electron genuinely has no single definite spin until measured, and the double-slit interference proves it, because both possibilities must be simultaneously real to interfere. Then you measure. The superposition abruptly resolves into exactly one outcome, and the odds of each are dictated by the wavefunction through the Born rule. The blend of possibilities becomes one fact.

## Why it matters

This is where quantum mechanics meets the everyday world, and it's the source of its deepest puzzle: why does measurement yield one crisp result when the math describes a smooth blend? The key correction to popular myth: "measurement" does *not* require a mind. It means any interaction that irreversibly entangles the system with a large environment — a detector, an air molecule, a photon — spreading the information around so that interference between the possibilities becomes impossible. This process, **decoherence**, explains why big objects never appear in superpositions: they're constantly "measured" by their surroundings. No consciousness required.

## The math (light)

A superposition writes the state as a weighted sum of the possible outcomes:

**|ψ⟩ = a|up⟩ + b|down⟩**

- **|up⟩, |down⟩** — the possible definite outcomes (here, spin results)
- **a, b** — amplitudes, numbers setting how much of each is in the mix
- **|a|², |b|²** — the probabilities of measuring "up" or "down," which must add to 1 (Born rule)

Before measurement both terms coexist; after, only one survives.

## See it

Schrödinger's famous cat dramatizes the tension: a cat's fate is tied to a single radioactive atom in superposition, so the math naively blends "alive" and "dead." The resolution isn't a magic observer — it's that a cat is enormously entangled with its environment and decoheres instantly, so it is only ever one or the other. The thought experiment's real lesson is *where* and *why* superpositions stop, not that cats hover between life and death.
